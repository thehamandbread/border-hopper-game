# Writing Style Guide

How Border Hopper is written. Applies to every line of dialogue, narration, hint, and text message.

## The medium
- The game is read, not heard. Sprites are small and can't act. Every emotional beat must survive as text alone.
- Narration lines (no speaker, muted color, typed slower) describe what the sprites can't show: actions, objects, silence. Narration is external, like a camera. It never states what anyone thinks or feels.
- Narration describes; it doesn't interpret. "[She sets the bottle down between them.]" not "[She wants him to explain.]"
- Choices go at the emotional peaks, not only at plot forks. Silence ("Say nothing.") is a real option where it fits.
- Pacing is a tool: slower text and held pauses for hesitation and weight.

## Core rules
1. No line exists only to deliver exposition. Every line is something that character would actually say, in that moment, to that person.
2. Trace what the player knows. Before using a name, place, or fact, check that the player has actually seen it on screen. Optional interactions don't count.
3. Subtext over statement. Characters rarely say what they feel. Emotion lives in what they avoid, what they change the subject to, and what they do.
4. Ordinary life keeps going. Mundane details (breakfast, being late, the news) carry scenes; the important thing sits underneath.
5. Beats before lines. Every scene starts as a beat sheet: what each character wants, hides, and feels; the turn; and what the player should feel at the end. Lines are written only after the beats are approved.

## Avoiding corny writing
- No punchlines. Don't end exchanges on a clever line.
- No signposted moments. Don't add narration that tells the player "this matters" (e.g., "[He doesn't say it like a joke.]").
- Not every line is witty. Real people are only occasionally funny, and usually not on purpose.
- End scenes early. If the scene works without its last line, cut it.
- No speeches. Long lines belong only to voices that call for them (Tomi excited, Mateo over-explaining), and even then they stay grounded in specifics.
- Prefer concrete details over general statements ("The whole page is just us" over "We owe a lot").
- Read it out loud. If it would feel strange to say, rewrite it.
- No lines that echo famous films or shows.

## Punctuation
- Ellipses (…) mean trailing off or searching.
- Dashes (—) mean cutting off or restarting.
- Use both sparingly.

## Spanish
- Light and natural, the way border-town speakers actually talk. Never translated in-line and never used as decoration. Meaning must be clear from context.
- The amount varies by character (see the voice sheets). Dichos (traditional sayings) are rare.

## Voice sheets

### Mateo
- Starting want: his family to be okay. His final desire is shaped by the player; the writing never assumes it.
- Core flaw: carries everything alone. Won't accept help; proud of handling things himself, even as it crushes him.
- Under pressure: quiet, not mute. Sentences shrink to fragments; he breaks off and restarts; he grabs practical things (numbers, times, tasks); he answers a different question than the one asked; he repeats himself to convince himself.
- When someone offers help or worries about him: he over-explains, listing what he's handled. The more he explains, the less sure he is.
- Humor: dry, mostly with Tomi and Marisol.
- With Marisol: full sentences, humor, laughing. His stress tells disappear. If they show up with her, something is badly wrong.
- Narration never shows his thoughts.

### Abuela (Esperanza)
- Old, wise, has seen a lot. Slow to trust; once she trusts someone, she trusts them completely. Reads people very well. Above all, tries never to be a burden.
- Speaks in short, complete, precise sentences. Warm word choices. No fragments, no rambling, no wasted words.
- Minimizes herself: "It's nothing. The stairs are steep, that's all."
- Reads people through small details, in one line: "You didn't take your jacket off when you came in."
- Notices more than she says, and waits. Narration carries her silences.
- Light Spanish ("mijo," "pobre"); dichos rarely.
- Arc: slowly sees what is happening to Mateo. Knew his father worked for Don Aurelio and never told him.

### Tomi
- 14, and thinks he's older. Fends for himself a lot and acts older than he is.
- Borrows adult phrases, especially Mateo's: "It's handled." "Don't worry about it." "I've got it."
- Reports hard things flatly, until he can't.
- When excited or scared, the kid breaks through: fast, run-on, emotional.
- When hurt: short and flat. "Fine. Whatever."
- Jokes when scared, then gives himself away with one short honest line.
- Calls Mateo "Teo" (the only one who does). Mixes in more casual Spanish than Mateo. Almost no slang.
- Flaw: impatience. Wants to grow up faster than he should.

### Don Aurelio
- Old-fashioned, old money, old respect. Courteous, unhurried, always in control of the conversation.
- Uses people's names and asks after their families by name. Gives respect first and expects it back.
- Never says "debt." Says "what is owed," "a favor," "an obligation."
- Rarely asks real questions; when he does, he already knows the answer. Answers sideways and gives less than he's asked for.
- Never threatens, never curses, never raises his voice. Hard truths come wrapped in courtesy or a favor.
- Complete, polished, slightly formal sentences.
- Texts are formal: full sentences, proper punctuation, no abbreviations.
- Measures people by old standards: punctuality, keeping your word, respect for elders.
- Occasionally nostalgic, briefly, never as a speech.
- His warmth toward Mateo is real, which is what makes him dangerous.
- Never a godfather impression.

### Rafael
- Everything his father isn't, on purpose. Efficient and impatient; short business sentences.
- Says "debt," "margin," "liability." Calls people by what they're for, not their names. Doesn't learn Mateo's name for a long time.
- Texts in fragments; always on his phone.
- Resents his father's warmth toward Mateo, and shows it as small contempt, not open anger.
- Underneath, hidden until late: a son who never got his father's respect and decided he didn't want it.

### Marisol
- Independent, driven, always on her way somewhere. Has her own life together and real plans; talks about the future.
- Doesn't need Mateo, which is why he's drawn to her.
- Direct; asks real questions and expects real answers. Notices physical things lightly (she's a nursing student) without trying to fix him.
- With Mateo: both of their lives are in constant motion, and they are each other's stopping point. Together they slow down, linger, talk about nothing, and feel safe. They start saying "we," and half-joking plans turn real.
- Mateo's lies are the one thing that can break that safe place.

### Minor characters
- Doña Lupe: the restaurant's owner. Offscreen in the prologue.
- Don Chuy: the pharmacist. Keeps the family's fiado notebook.
